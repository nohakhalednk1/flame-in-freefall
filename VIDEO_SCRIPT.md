# Flame in Freefall · Video script

Check the 2026 Project Submission Guide for the exact length allowed.
Two versions are below: a 30-second cut and a 2-minute cut. Both use screen
recordings of the website, so record in a dark room with the browser full screen.

Tip: record the screen with OBS or the Windows Game Bar (Win + G), and the
voice separately on a phone, then join them in CapCut or Clipchamp.

## Version B · about 2 minutes

**1. Hook (0:00–0:15)**
Screen: hero, slow scroll to the stats.
Voice: "On Earth, you know when a fire is out. In space, you might not.
NASA has lit hundreds of fires on the International Space Station on purpose
to learn why. We are team [TEAM NAME], and this is Flame in Freefall."

**2. Why space fire is different (0:15–0:30)**
Screen: "Same candle, two worlds" animation.
Voice: "Without gravity, hot air does not rise. A candle flame turns from a tall
teardrop into a dim blue ball, and fire starts to follow different rules."

**3. The invisible flame (0:30–0:55)**
Screen: replay animation. Let it run to 12.6 s, pause, switch on Infrared view, play.
Voice: "This is real ISS data from a 4 millimetre fuel droplet. At 12.6 seconds
the bright flame disappears. A crew member would call it out. But turn on
infrared: a cool flame keeps burning for 33 more seconds, more than two and a
half times longer than the fire we could see."

**4. It can come back (0:55–1:10)**
Screen: "The fire that comes back" chart, point at stage 3.
Voice: "In another run, the flame died at 8 seconds, burned invisibly, then
re-ignited by itself at 26 seconds, hotter than before."

**5. Real spacecraft fires (1:10–1:30)**
Screen: Saffire photo, bars, material cards, then the CO chart.
Voice: "Inside an uncrewed cargo ship, NASA set large fires. Airflow pushing the
flame forward made fabric burn six times longer. Nomex fabric never ignited.
And thick plastic, burning quietly for up to 15 minutes, released about 21
times more carbon monoxide than thin film."

**6. From data to decisions (1:30–1:50)**
Screen: fire drill, one wrong and one right answer; then the material risk check.
Voice: "We turned every result into tools: a fire drill for a lunar habitat,
and a material risk check that says plainly when there is no data."

**7. Close (1:50–2:00)**
Screen: safety insight cards, then sources section, then hero.
Voice: "Every chart and every recommendation links back to NASA's open PSI
data. Flame in Freefall: so the next fire in space never hides from its crew."

## Project summary (for the submission form, about 100 words)

Flame in Freefall turns NASA's microgravity combustion data into an interactive
story about fire safety for the Moon and Mars. Using open data from NASA's
Physical Sciences Informatics repository (PSI-117 droplet combustion, Saffire-1
and -2, BASS-II), we show that flames can look extinguished while a cool flame
keeps burning 2.6× longer, can re-ignite by themselves, burn 6× longer when
airflow runs along them, and release up to 21× more carbon monoxide from thick
plastics. A data-driven replay, a fire drill and a material risk check turn
each finding into a decision a crew can practise. A Python pipeline cleans the
raw files; every chart cites its source.
